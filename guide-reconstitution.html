Handling guide HG-001

Peptide reconstitution and storage

What to do between opening the vial and running the assay: equilibration, solvent choice from the sequence, dissolving without loss, aliquoting, storage and quantification.

This guide describes general laboratory practice for handling synthetic peptides as research reagents. It is not a protocol for any particular experiment and gives no guidance on amounts or concentrations, which are the researcher's decisions. For laboratory research use only. Not for human or veterinary use.

1.Before you open the vial

Lyophilised peptides are hygroscopic. A cold vial opened in room air condenses water onto the powder, which changes its mass and starts hydrolysis. Let the sealed vial stand at room temperature for 20 to 30 minutes, then spin it briefly in a benchtop centrifuge so that any powder on the walls or stopper collects at the bottom. Only then remove the cap. Weigh out quickly if you are weighing, and reseal the remainder under desiccant.

The certificate of analysis for your batch states the purity by HPLC and, where measured, the net peptide content. Lyophilised peptide is never 100% peptide by mass: bound water and the trifluoroacetate counter-ion that remains from purification typically account for 10 to 30% of the weight.[1] Use the net peptide content when the molar amount matters.

2.Choosing a solvent

The sequence predicts the behaviour. Count the charged residues at neutral pH: lysine, arginine and histidine on the positive side, aspartate and glutamate on the negative side, and remember the free N-terminus and C-terminus unless they are capped. Then count the hydrophobic residues. The starting points below are the ones most often reported for peptides of each type.[2] Try a small portion first, and always add the organic solvent before the aqueous buffer, never the other way round.

Net positive, mostly polarSterile water or buffer. If the peptide does not dissolve, 10% acetic acid, then dilute with buffer.
Net negative, mostly polarSterile water or buffer. If needed, 0.1 M ammonium bicarbonate or a little dilute ammonia, then dilute.
Neutral or more than half hydrophobicDissolve in the smallest volume of DMSO (or acetonitrile) that wets the powder, then add buffer slowly with mixing. Keep the final DMSO below 1% for cell work.
Contains cysteineUse degassed solvent and work promptly. Free thiols oxidise to disulfides in air, especially at neutral or alkaline pH.
Contains methionine or tryptophanAvoid oxidising conditions and strong light. Store stocks under nitrogen or argon if they will be kept.
Known to aggregate (for example amyloid β)Start from a monomeric film: dissolve in HFIP, evaporate, then take up in DMSO immediately before use, as described in the monograph.[3]

3.Dissolving

Add the solvent slowly down the inside wall of the vial so that it wets the powder from below rather than splashing it onto the cap. Swirl or invert gently. Vigorous vortexing foams the solution and can denature longer peptides, and it costs material on the walls. If the powder does not clear, sonicate in a water bath for a minute or two, warming no higher than 40 °C, and spin down before use. A faint haze after that usually means aggregation or a poor solvent choice, not an insoluble batch, so return to section 2 before adding more of anything.

Work out the concentration from the mass and the volume you actually added, not from the nominal vial size if you weighed out a portion. The calculator does this arithmetic and converts between mass, volume and molar units.

4.Aliquots and storage

Peptides in solution are far less stable than the dry powder. Divide the stock into single-use aliquots in low-binding tubes, label them with the concentration and date, and freeze at −20 °C, or −80 °C for longer storage. Each freeze–thaw cycle costs purity, so never refreeze a thawed aliquot. Solutions at neutral pH, and any that contain cysteine, methionine, tryptophan, asparagine or glutamine, deserve the shorter end of any storage time you allow.[4]

Keep the dry powder at −20 °C in the original sealed vial with desiccant and away from light. Stored that way, most sequences are stable for years. Let it return to room temperature before every opening, as in section 1.

5.Quantifying the solution

If the sequence contains tryptophan or tyrosine, you can check the concentration of the stock by absorbance at 280 nm using the molar extinction coefficient: 5,500 M⁻¹cm⁻¹ per tryptophan and 1,490 M⁻¹cm⁻¹ per tyrosine, with 125 M⁻¹cm⁻¹ for each cystine.[5] Each monograph in the library lists the value for its sequence. Peptides without aromatic residues are quantified at 214 nm against a standard, by amino acid analysis, or by weight corrected for net peptide content.

6.Worked example

A vial labelled 5 mg of a 1,419.55 g/mol peptide is reconstituted in 2 mL. The concentration is 5 mg ÷ 2 mL = 2.5 mg/mL, which is 2.5 µg/µL, or 2.5 g/L ÷ 1,419.55 g/mol = 1.76 mM. A sample that is to contain 250 µg needs 250 ÷ 2.5 = 100 µL, which reads as 10 units on a U-100 syringe scale (1 unit = 10 µL), and the vial yields twenty such samples. If the certificate gives a net peptide content of 85%, the peptide actually present is 4.25 mg and every figure scales by 0.85. Open this example in the calculator. The numbers are illustrative and are not a recommendation of any amount.
